Section 03 · Sector assessment

US fitness demand is at a record and broadening; the middle tier wins if it holds price between a discounting HVLP tier and a premium tier

This tab reads the health club sector for an owner and a lender: demand, the GLP-1 question, price and format, unit economics, and the sector's credit history. Every figure is public, with its source and date.

A record 81 million Americans belong to a gym, about 6% more each year since 2023, with both the young and the old joining

Penetration reached 26.1% of Americans aged six and over in 2025, up more than 600 bps in a decade. The two fastest-growing groups sit at either end of the age range: Gen Z adults, with the highest penetration of any group, and members over 65. Both favour clubs with pools, classes and recovery over a bare box.

Americans spent $85bn at clubs and sports centres in 2025, 39% more than in 2019, but growth slowed to 4% and membership prices have started to fall

The government's own figures back up the industry's. Spending and jobs are at records. Two numbers point the other way in 2026: the price index for club memberships is down 1.3% on a year earlier, the first fall in years, and consumer confidence is at a historic low. Both argue for watching price and promotions weekly.

People on weight-loss drugs are joining gyms, not quitting, and most new members went to the cheapest or the most premium clubs

William Blair's 2026 survey shows gym membership among GLP-1 users rising from 24% to 35% in two years. More than 60% of member growth since 2019 went to high-value, low-price clubs, Planet Fitness alone taking 42%. In 2025, HVLP clubs led on visits, mid-priced clubs grew and luxury clubs fell in most regions.

Older Americans are a quiet growth engine: 61 million people are over 65, and 93% of Medicare Advantage plans include a gym benefit

Members over 65 grew faster than any other age group in 2025. They come in the daytime, when big clubs are quiet, they value pools and classes, and Medicare Advantage plans increasingly pay for access. For a club like Onelife that is extra revenue in hours that are otherwise empty.

The market is splitting: Planet Fitness has stopped growing at its existing clubs while Life Time raises dues 13%, and Onelife sits in between

Planet Fitness cut its 2026 same-club sales guide from 4–5% to about 1% in May, citing competition, and paused its Black Card increase. Life Time grew dues 13.3% in Q2 2026 on 1.2% more memberships, all price and mix. A mid-tier club with pools and recovery at $40–50 a month is priced against the first and equipped against the second.

A big mid-priced club costs three to five times a budget gym to build and has to earn that back through extras

The HVLP model runs about 20k sq ft for about $3m and earns 35–42% at the four walls on $1.7m of sales. Mid-tier big boxes such as Onelife's run 50–66k sq ft for $11–18m. They clear the same returns only with two to three times the revenue per club, which comes from higher dues, attached tiers and ancillary spend.

The big gym failures of 2020 had one cause in common: fixed rent and heavy debt when revenue fell to zero

24 Hour Fitness, Gold's Gym and Town Sports all filed in 2020. Each used Chapter 11 to reject leases in bulk. The lesson for a lender or owner today is less about pandemics than about lease-adjusted leverage and the speed at which members stop paying when clubs close.

For Onelife the sector looks good: demand is at a record, the middle is growing, and the risks show up early on a weekly board

The sector question for 26North is not whether people go to gyms; it is whether a mid-tier operator can hold price between a discounting HVLP tier and a premium tier that keeps raising dues. That is a weekly question about yield, mix and churn, not an annual one.

Section 04 · Peers and capital markets

Onelife is a rare owned mid-tier platform in a sector private credit is actively financing, valued in line with its closest peers

This tab sets Onelife against its peers on format, price and scale; against the listed comparables on multiples and leverage; against eight recent deals and financings; and against the competitors in each of its states.

Onelife is one of very few mid-priced chains that owns all its big clubs; most rivals are budget franchises or premium resorts

The closest operating peers are LA Fitness, 24 Hour Fitness and the big-box HVLP chains (EoS, VASA). The closest financial peers, the platforms private credit has recently lent to, are Crunch, EoS and GoodLife.

The two US-listed gym chains went opposite ways in 2026: Planet Fitness stalled, Life Time kept growing

Planet Fitness traded at 17.4x LTM EBITDA at the end of 2025 and roughly 10x 2026 EBITDA after its May reset (CreditSwan estimate). Life Time trades near 8.5x on Houlihan Lokey's basis. European multi-site operators cluster at 8x with leverage near 5x, the closest public read on a mid-tier owner-operator.

Private lenders have funded every large gym deal since late 2024, and reported prices cluster around $8–9m per owned club

Golub financed both Crunch and EoS; Ares and JPMorgan led GoodLife; TCW refinanced 24 Hour Fitness. The market is open for scaled club platforms, which matters both for Onelife's eventual exit and for how 26North's own direct lending team reads the sector.

In its home markets Onelife sits between Life Time above and dense budget gyms below, and Georgia is about to get more crowded

Planet Fitness alone has roughly five times Onelife's club count in Georgia. Life Time has seven clubs in Virginia, four in Maryland and five in North Carolina, three of them in the Triangle where Onelife is building. The competitive monitors on the board watch these overlaps weekly.

Rivals point to three things to watch every week: prices against budget-gym offers, how new clubs fill up, and debt including rent

Each lesson below is drawn from a peer's record and turned into a monitor or a covenant question.

Section 05 · Onelife Fitness

Onelife is a mature, cash-generative Mid-Atlantic club estate funding a move up-market; the ramp of its new clubs is the variable that matters

Fifty-seven clubs opened before 2024 carry the economics. The fourteen opened since, and about $132m of named projects, decide whether 26North's growth case compounds or dilutes. All operating figures are illustrative (A-02); the deal facts are public and tagged.

26North bought control of Onelife in October 2024 from Delos Capital, which had co-owned it with Josh Harris's family office since 2018

This is a whole-company platform buyout, not a carve-out: the team, systems and brand came over intact, which is why the 100-day question is about pace and returns on new clubs rather than standalone separation. Terms were not disclosed; the only value reported is about $500m (Bloomberg), against a $700m ask in the 2023 process.

Note · related party

Harris's family office was a seller-side co-owner and Harris's firm the buyer. Whether HRS sold or rolled is not public; an LP or lender reading the deal will ask, so the board keeps the pre- and post-close series on one basis.

Caution · value

The $500m figure is reported, not disclosed, and may be equity rather than enterprise value. If it is enterprise value it is about 7x the 2023 projected EBITDA, below the $700m asked a year earlier.

Four in five Onelife clubs are in Virginia, Georgia and Maryland; the new growth is in states where it has no clubs yet

The Mid-Atlantic core is mature and dense; North Carolina and Alabama are greenfield, with a plan for 15+ clubs in the Triangle and five in Alabama. New-state clubs ramp without brand awareness or a nearby club for members to transfer to, which is what the board watches first.

Washington DC, Onelife's biggest market, lost more jobs than any other US metro last year as federal payrolls were cut

More than a third of the clubs sit in a metro that shed 103,900 jobs between January 2025 and January 2026, a fall of 3.1%. Federal employment there fell about 14%. Unemployment is still low, at 3.8% in August 2026, and household incomes remain the highest in the footprint, so the risk is slower joins and more freezes in Northern Virginia and Maryland rather than a collapse. The board watches DC clubs as their own group for that reason.

Caution · the biggest market

A third of the clubs and probably more of the profit sit in the DC area. A live board splits joins, freezes and cancellations for DC clubs so a federal-cuts effect shows up weeks before it reaches the monthly accounts.

Note · where the growth is

Raleigh and Huntsville grew about four times faster than DC over five years and have one and two clubs. If new-club returns hold up, these are the obvious places to put the next conversions.

Membership has grown with the club count, but what members pay depends on which tier they choose

At list prices a Premier or STRIKE member is worth 1.25x and 2.5x a Basic member. The model holds the member base at about 424k at Sep-26 and an effective dues rate of $44.10 against a $49.50 list mix, the gap being rate-locked legacy members and joining promotions.

Members rate the Washington clubs well, but online complaints cluster on the annual fee, training bills and how hard it is to cancel

Independent ratings are strong where Onelife is oldest: up to 87% of surveyed members rate a club superior. The weak spots are the same three that brought a federal lawsuit against LA Fitness in 2025: surprise fees, billing and cancellation. Prices on core-market club pages are also higher than the Alabama prices the model was built on, so the gap between list and paid prices may be larger than the model assumes.

Caution · price paid

The model assumes list prices from an Alabama club. Core clubs list Premier at $62.99, not $49.99, which would raise revenue if members actually pay it, or mean deeper discounts if they do not. Pulling the billing file settles this in the first week.

Note · no Medicare programme

The club pages we checked in Oct 2026 mention IncentFit and Les Mills but no SilverSneakers or Renew Active link. Over-65s are one of the fastest-growing groups of gym members (tab 03), so this is a cheap lever worth confirming.

A mature Onelife club keeps about a third of its revenue as profit; clubs opened since 2024 still lose money or break even

The model places 57 mature clubs at a 31–36% four-wall margin on $4.1–4.9m of revenue, and fourteen clubs opened since 2024 at a combined margin near break-even. That is the normal shape of a growing estate; the question for the board is whether each new vintage is ramping faster or slower than the last.

Onelife has announced about $132m of new clubs since 2025, before the Tysons flagship, and is moving up-market

The named projects alone are roughly 1.5x the model's four-wall EBITDA. The move to 60k+ sq ft clubs with pools, Pilates and recovery suites brings Onelife closer to Life Time's format at a third of Life Time's price, which works only if Premier and STRIKE attach keeps rising.

Six questions decide how much of the growth plan is already in the price, and each is tracked on the weekly board

These are the questions CreditSwan would put to management in the first data pull. Each has a source system that answers it and a monitor that keeps it answered every week.

More people are joining than leaving, but more of the new joiners are leaving fast

Headline membership looks healthy. The early-life cancellation rate, the share of joins that cancel inside 90 days, is the series that broke, and it broke in the new states.

Members are paying a little less each week, and a billing change has caused a spike in failed payments

Neither is large on its own: the dues drift is worth about $0.6m a year at today's base, and the excess failed drafts about $0.3m a month until fixed. Both are the kind of leak a quarterly pack shows a quarter late.

Caution · the $1 offer is illustrative

The board's promotion story (a $1 join offer pulling joiners down to Basic) is a demo scenario. Onelife's club pages in October 2026 showed free guest passes and a $5 Premier join fee, not a $1 offer. A live board reads the actual promotion calendar from the billing system.

Note · list prices vary by market

Core-market clubs list Basic at $49.99 and Premier at $62.99, about $10 above the Alabama prices the model uses (Exhibit 5.26). The yield monitors compare each club with its own list price, so this does not change the alerts.

The 2025 openings are at 82% of the members they were built for, and three named clubs explain the gap

Club-level monitors are where the board earns its keep for a growth plan: each opening is tracked against the members it was underwritten to have at the same age.

Every problem arrives with its cause, the proof, an owner and a next step, so Monday starts with decisions

The diagnosis is written from the same source data as the monitor, so the cause is evidenced rather than asserted. Owners are roles, set when the board is stood up.

Every measure reads a named company system, so Monday's number is the number in the books

The board is only as good as its definitions. These are fixed when the board is stood up and versioned; a change of definition shows on the board as a break in the series, never silently.

Section 07 · 100-day plan and platform

The board becomes a 100-day plan tool for the Alpha Creation Team, an early-warning line for lenders and one evidence base across 26North

This tab shows how an operating partner would use the board: to run a 100-day plan by measured workstream, to size the levers in the existing estate, to gate the capital programme, and to give 26North's credit and insurance businesses the same evidence. Figures are illustrative where marked.

The board turns a 100-day plan into weekly proof: every workstream has a measure, an owner and a limit from day one

Onelife is two years into 26North's ownership, so this is a reset plan for the next 100 days rather than a post-close plan. The same structure applies on day one of a new holding or a carve-out such as Composition Brands. The Alpha Creation Team owns the plan; the board tells it every Monday whether the plan is working.

Seven fixes the board can measure are worth about $17m a year of EBITDA in the model, and the biggest is helping clubs already open to fill up

None of these needs a new club. They are the gains in the existing estate that a weekly board makes visible and a quarterly pack makes late. The sizes are illustrative and rest on A-02; the ranking is the point.

Nine new clubs open by the end of 2027, and the two largest, Richmond and Tysons, come last

The pipeline is public; the budgets mostly are not. The board tracks each opening against schedule and budget (M25, M26), and the capex gate in the 100-day plan decides whether the 2027 builds proceed at their announced scope.

For a lender, the same board is an early warning: on an illustrative loan there is a 20% cushion, and members quitting eats it fastest

This is the view for Brendan McGovern's team, or for any lender to a 26North portfolio company: the monitors that sit behind each covenant, read weekly from the systems the underwriting used. The capital structure is illustrative because Onelife's debt is not disclosed (A-09).

One set of evidence serves all three 26North businesses: the deal team, the lenders and 26North Re
